\begin{abstract}
This documentation presents numerical studies for the 2D heat conduction equation. The heat equation is solved using classical numerical methods, such as explicit Euler and implicit Crank-Nicolson, as well as Physics Informed Neural Networks (PINNs). Comparisons between these methods are provided, highlighting stability, accuracy, and computational efficiency.
\end{abstract}

\section{The classical Heat Equation}
\subsection{The Energy Balance and the Derivation of the Heat Equation}
We now derive the heat equation from the energy balance. For further reading see \cite[pp 207]{eck2008modellierung} and \cite[pp 105]{hoffmann2014modellierung}
Heat conduction describes the transfer of thermal energy in materials and is governed by the heat equation. The mechanism of heat conduction takes places on the scale of atoms and molecules and their local motion as mechanism to transfer heat. 
We consider an open spatial domain $\Omega \subset \mathbbm{R}^d $ where the heat conduction is investigated. 
Let $R \subset \Omega $ be an arbitrary subdomain of $\Omega $ with the following assumptions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Regularity:} $R$ is regular, so that all following integrals and operations on $R$ are well-defined.
\item \textbf{Absence of particle motion:} $R$ does not move over time, i.e. macroscopic motion of particles inside $R$ is not allowed.
\end{enumerate}
The heat equation can be derived from energy balance as follows. The change in time of the internal energy $\mathscr{E}(R) $ is given by the heat flux crossing the boundary of $R$ and the heat sources and sinks in $R$, i.e.
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} \mathscr{E} (R) = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} \int_R \varrho u \mathrm{d} V = \int_R \varrho f \mathrm{d} V - \int_{\partial R } \boldsymbol{q}\cdot \boldsymbol{\nu}_{\partial R} \mathrm{d} A .
\end{equation*}
The used symbols have the following meaning:

Since $R$ is regular we can interchange time derivation and integration and apply the Gauss-Green theorem on the boundary integral to obtain
\begin{equation*}
\int_R \tfrac{\mathrm{\partial}}{\partial t}  \left( \varrho u \right) \mathrm{d} V = \int_R \left( \varrho f  -  \mathrm{div}(\boldsymbol{q}) \right) \mathrm{d} V , \text{ or }
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
\frac{1}{\mathrm{meas}(R)}\int_R \tfrac{\mathrm{\partial}}{\partial t}  \left( \varrho u \right) \mathrm{d} V = \frac{1}{\mathrm{meas}(R)} \int_R \left( \varrho f  -  \mathrm{div}(\boldsymbol{q}) \right) \mathrm{d} V.
\end{equation*}
Since $R$ is an arbitrary sample volume, we can drop the integration to get
\begin{equation*}
\tfrac{\mathrm{\partial}}{\partial t}  \left( \varrho u \right) = \varrho f  -  \mathrm{div}(\boldsymbol{q}) .
\end{equation*}
We now fix the following assumptions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(A1)] Heat conduction is the only mechanism of heat transfer. That means convection and radiation are not present.
\item[(A2)] Heat sources and sinks in the bulk are nor present.
\item[(A3)] The specific internal energy is given by $u = c_P \cdot\Temp$, where $c_P$ is the specific heat capacity at constant pressure and $T$ is the temperature.
\item[(A4)] The heat flux per area is given by $ \boldsymbol{q} = - \kappa \nabla T $ (Fourier's law), where $\kappa $ denotes the thermal conductivity.
\item[(A5)] The mass density $\varrho $ and the specific heat capacity $c_P$ do not change over time.
\item[(A6)] The thermal conductivity $\kappa $ is a constant in space.
\end{enumerate}
These assumptions lead to the classical heat conduction equation given by
\begin{equation}
\varrho c_P \frac{\partial \Temp}{\partial t} = \kappa \Laplace \Temp , \text{ or }
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \alpha \Laplace \Temp \text{ in } \Omega ,
\end{equation}
with the thermal diffusivity $\alpha = \kappa / c_P / \varrho $. The following table assigns the physical units to the quantities just introduced.

\textbf{General Assumption:} From now on we assume that $\Omega $ is a  2D quadratic domain $\Omega = (0,1)\times (0,1)$. Then the classical heat equation can be written as
\begin{equation} \label{HeatEquation2D}
\frac{\partial \Temp}{\partial t} = \alpha \left( \frac{\partial^2 \Temp}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \Temp}{\partial y^2} \right) + f = \alpha \Laplace \Temp + f .
\end{equation}

\subsection{Initial and Boundary Conditions}
For a complete well-posed the heat conduction problem (\ref{HeatEquation2D}) has to be completed with initial and boundary values. Robin boundary conditions cover Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions, i.e.
\begin{equation*}
a \Temp + b \frac{\partial \Temp}{\partial \nu } = c, \text{ or } \gamma (\Temp - \Temp_b ) + \frac{\partial \Temp}{\partial \nu } = 0, \text{ on }\partial \Omega \times (0,T)
\end{equation*}
for solutions $\Temp $ of $ \partial \Temp / \partial t = \alpha\Laplace \Temp +f $ and $a \Temp_b = c, \; \gamma = a/b $.
\begin{remark}
From now on we \emph{always assume} $ \gamma > 0 $. That is important to prove some of the essential results in the following section.
\end{remark}

A typical initial condition which will be used here is $ \Temp (x,y,0)= \Temp_0 (x, y) $. 

\section{Eigenfunctions and Green's Function}

The idea of Green's function is to provide an exact solution which  fulfills (\ref{HeatEquation2D}) subject to initial and boundary condition. For simple domains $\Omega $ an analytical expression can be calculated which can be used to calculate values of $ \Temp $ at every spatial point $(x,y) \in \Omega $ to any time $t>0 $. For difficult domains it is not possible to derive a useful analytical expression for the Green's function that allows for value evaluations of $\Temp$. Nevertheless Green's function allow to get insights and estimates for a solution. Since the solution of a well-posed problem is unique, insights, e.g. a-priori estimates and regularity results from Green's function are valid for the solution of the problem no matter in which way this solution has been calculated.

\subsection{The homogeneous Heat Conduction Problem}
To construct a Green's function first a particular solution of the homogenous problem, i.e.
\begin{eqnarray} \label{homogenHeatC}
\frac{\partial \wTemp }{\partial t } &=& \alpha\Laplace \wTemp  , \text{ in } \Omega , \\
\wTemp (x,y,0 ) & = & \wTemp_0(x,y) \label{homogenIC} , \\
\gamma \wTemp + \frac{\partial \wTemp }{\partial \nu } &=& 0 \text{ on } \partial \Omega  \label{homogenBC} .
\end{eqnarray}
has to be constructed. For this purpose we closely follow \cite[p. 50 f]{meinhold1990pdg}.

First we apply a product approach $\wTemp (x,y,t) = \Psi (x,y) \cdot \Theta (t) $ for $ \wTemp $. Then (\ref{homogenHeatC}) turns into $ \Psi (x,y) \cdot \Theta' (t) = \alpha\Laplace \Psi (x,y) \cdot \Theta (t) $ which can be rewritten as
\begin{equation}\label{homogenHeatC1}
\frac{\Theta' (t)}{\alpha \Theta (t) } = \frac{\Laplace \Psi (x,y) }{\Psi (x,y) } = -\Lambda .
\end{equation}
Equation (\ref{homogenHeatC1}) can be fulfilled, only if every quotient therein is constant and hence $ \Lambda $ is a constant. This leads to two eigenvalue problems:
\begin{eqnarray} 
\Theta' (t) = -\alpha \Lambda \Theta (t) , \text{ and } \label{eigenvalsODE}\\
\Laplace \Psi (x,y) = -\Lambda \Psi (x,y) . \label{eigenvalsLaplace}
\end{eqnarray}
We skip the case $ \Lambda < 0 $ and classical solution theory of linear ordinary differential equations leads to the solution $ \Theta (t) = A_0 \mathrm{exp}(-\alpha\Lambda (t-t_0)) $ of (\ref{eigenvalsODE}). With $t_0 = 0$ this is $ \Theta (t) = A_0 \mathrm{exp}(-\alpha\Lambda t) $ for $ \Lambda > 0 $. For $ \Lambda = 0$ the equation (\ref{eigenvalsODE}) is solved by a constant. 

We are now going to solve (\ref{eigenvalsLaplace}). Again we adopt a product approach $ \Psi (x,y) = X(x) \cdot Y(y) $ which is reasonable for a cartesian-like domain $ \Omega = (0,1) \times (0,1) $. Then (\ref{eigenvalsLaplace}) turns into $ X''(x) Y(y) +X(x) Y''(y) = -\Lambda X(x)Y(y) $, or
\begin{equation} \label{eigenvalsLaplace2}
\frac{X''(x)}{X(x)} + \frac{Y''(y)}{Y(y)} = - \Lambda .
\end{equation}
Every summand in (\ref{eigenvalsLaplace2}) must be a constant, thus
\begin{equation}  \label{eigenvalsLaplace3}
\frac{X''(x)}{X(x)} = -\mu^2, \text{ and } \frac{Y''(y)}{Y(y)} = -\lambda^2 ,\text{ and } \mu^2 + \lambda^2 = \Lambda ,
\end{equation}
with constants $\mu $ and $\lambda $. The equations (\ref{eigenvalsLaplace3}) describe two Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue problems, cf. \cite[p. 285]{walter2000ode}, precisely we have
\begin{equation} \label{sturm_liou_X}
X''(x) + \mu^2 X(x) = 0 \text{ and }\gamma X(0) - X'(0) = 0 \text{ and }\gamma X(1) + X'(1) = 0,
\end{equation}
for  $X = X(x) $, and
\begin{equation} \label{sturm_liou_Y}
Y''(y) + \lambda^2 Y(y) = 0\text{ and } \gamma Y(0) - Y'(0) = 0 \text{ and }\gamma Y(1) + Y'(1) = 0,
\end{equation}
for $Y = Y(y) $. We now a state a first result, that is
\begin{lemma}[Eigenvalues of the Sturm-Liouville problem] \label{sturm_liou__eig_vals}
For the Sturm-Liouville problems (\ref{sturm_liou_X}) and (\ref{sturm_liou_Y}) there exist infinitely many eigenvalues $ \mu^2 $ and $ \lambda^2 $. For the sequences of eigenvalues $ \{ \mu^2_n \}_{n \geq 0 } $ of (\ref{sturm_liou_X}) and $ \{ \lambda^2_n \}_{n \geq 0 } $ of (\ref{sturm_liou_Y}) we have 
\begin{equation} \label{eig_monotone}
0 \leq \mu^2_0 < \dots < \mu^2_m < \mu^2_{m+1} < \dots \text{ and }\; 0 \leq \lambda^2_0 < \dots < \lambda^2_n < \lambda^2_{n+1} < \dots ,
\end{equation}
for all $m$, $n \in \mathbbm{N} $. Furthermore, the sequences have the (improper) limits
\begin{equation} \label{eig_limit}
\mathrm{lim}_{n\rightarrow\infty } \mu^2_n = +\infty \text{ and }\; \mathrm{lim}_{n\rightarrow\infty } \lambda^2_n = +\infty .
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
%
For the proof we use techniques of the spectral theory of linear operators. For brevity we cannot go in detail here. Essentials on spectral theory can be found in every good textbook on functional analysis, for example in \cite[pp 130 f]{meinhold1990pdg}, or in \cite[pp 465–505, pp 542–571]{heuser1986funktionalanalysis}.
\begin{definition}[Spectrum and resolvent set]
For a linear operator $A \in \mathscr{L} (H) $ on a Hilbert space $H$ we define the \emph{resolvent set} $\varrho (A) $ as the subset of the complex plane $\mathbbm{C}$ containing all $ z \in \mathbbm{C}$ for which $ A - zI $ has a continuous inverse $(A - zI)^{-1} \in \mathscr{L} (H) $. The complement set $\sigma (A) = \mathbbm{C}\setminus \varrho (A)$ is called the \emph{spectrum} of $A$. The operator $R(z,A) = (A - zI)^{-1} $ is called \emph{resolvent}.
\end{definition}
\begin{proof}%[Sketch of the proof]
We define a linear operator $ A : H^2(\Omega ) \rightarrow L^2(\Omega ) $ via the sesquilinear functional $\ell : H^2(\Omega ) \times H^2(\Omega ) \rightarrow \mathbbm{C}$ given by the expression
\begin{comment}
\begin{eqnarray*}
\ell (w, \xi ) & = & \left( A w, \xi \right)_{L^2(\Omega ) } \\
& = & \left( \nabla w, \nabla \xi \right)_{L^2(\Omega ,\mathbbm{R}^d ) } - \lambda^2 \left( w, \xi \right)_{L^2(\Omega ,\mathbbm{R} ) } + \gamma \left( w, \xi \right)_{L^2(\partial \Omega ,\mathbbm{R} ) } \\
& = & \int_\Omega \left( \nabla w \cdot \nabla \xi - \lambda^2 w \xi \right) \mathrm{d}\mu + \gamma \int_{\partial \Omega } w \xi \mathrm{d} S .
\end{eqnarray*}
\end{comment}
%
\begin{eqnarray}
\ell (w, \xi ) & = & \left( A w, \xi \right)_{L^2(\Omega ) } \label{ell_func_1}\\
& = & \left( \nabla w, \nabla \xi \right)_{L^2(\Omega ,\mathbbm{R}^d ) } + \gamma \left( w, \xi \right)_{L^2(\partial \Omega ,\mathbbm{R} ) } \label{ell_func_2}\\
& = & \int_\Omega \nabla w \cdot \nabla \xi  \mathrm{d}\mu + \gamma \int_{\partial \Omega } w \xi \mathrm{d} S .
\end{eqnarray}
Note that we could have chosen $H^1(\Omega ) $ instead of $H^2(\Omega ) $. By definition, the operator $A$ is self-adjoint and for all $ z \in \mathbbm{C} $. The operator $ A -z I $ maps $ H^2(\Omega ) $ into $ L^2(\Omega ) $ as $A$ and is self-adjoint too. Then, its resolvent $R(z,A ) = (A -z I )^{-1} $ for $ z \in \varrho (A) $ is a linear mapping from $ L^2(\Omega ) $ to  $H^2(\Omega )$. According to standard theory of Sobolev spaces the embedding $j: H^2(\Omega ) \rightarrow L^2(\Omega ) $ is a compact operator, hence $ j \circ R(z,A ) : L^2(\Omega ) \rightarrow L^2(\Omega ) $ is compact, too. Redefining $ R(z,A )= j \circ R(z,A ) $ the resolvent identity $R(z,A ) - R(z_0,A ) = (z_0 - z) R(z,A ) R(z_0,A )$ assures compact $ R(z,A) $ for all $ z \in \varrho (A) $. Next we apply \cite[Theorem 15.12, p 137]{meinhold1990pdg} that allows to write
\begin{equation*}
R(z,A )\psi_n = \zeta_n \psi_n \text{ and } \psi_n = \zeta_n (A - zI) \psi_n \text{ for all } z \in \varrho (A)
\end{equation*}
with a sequence $ \{ \zeta_n \}_{n \in \mathbbm{N}} $ of complex numbers of $\sigma (R(z,A )) $ tending to zero as $n$ tends to infinity and eigenfunctions $\psi_n $ attached to $ \zeta_n $. Vice versa, $ z_n = 1 / \zeta_n $ define the eigenvalues of $(A - zI) $, i.e.
\begin{equation*}
(A - zI) \psi_n = z_n \psi_n \text{ and } A \psi_n = \eta_n \psi_n ,
\end{equation*}
where $ \eta_n = z + z_n $. Since $ \zeta_n $ has zero limit $ \eta_n $ tends to infinity as $n \rightarrow \infty $. Note that $ \eta_n \in \sigma (A) $ and since $A$ is self-adjoint we have $ \eta_n \in \sigma (A) \subset \mathbbm{R}$. Inserting $ w = \psi_n $ and $ \xi = \psi_n $ into (\ref{ell_func_1}) we have
\begin{equation*}
\int_\Omega \left| \nabla \psi_n \right|^2  \mathrm{d}\mu + \gamma \int_{\partial \Omega } \left| \psi_n \right|^2 \mathrm{d} S = \eta_n \int_\Omega \left| \psi_n \right|^2 \mathrm{d} \mu \geq 0,
\end{equation*}
that is $ \eta_n \geq 0 $ and $ \eta_n = \mu^2_n $ is justified for $ \mu_n \in \mathbbm{R} $. The proof of (\ref{eig_monotone}) and (\ref{eig_limit}) is done.
\end{proof}

We are now going to match the boundary conditions (\ref{homogenBC}) for the solution $X$. For $w (x,y,t) = X(x)\cdot Y(y) \cdot \Theta (t) $ (\ref{homogenBC}) reduces to
\begin{equation*}
\gamma X(x) + \frac{\partial X(x) }{\partial \nu } = 0 \text{ on } \{ 0,1\}\times (0,1) .
\end{equation*}
The normal derivative $ \partial X / \partial \nu $ on $ \{ 0,1\}\times (0,1) $ is given by
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\partial X(x) }{\partial \nu } =
\begin{cases}
-X'(0) & \text{ on } \{ 0\}\times (0,1), \\
X'(1) & \text{ on } \{ 1\}\times (0,1) ,
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
and the boundary condition as
\begin{equation}
0 = \gamma X(x) + \frac{\partial X(x) }{\partial \nu } =
\begin{cases}
\gamma B_2 - \mu B_1  & \text{ for } \mu \not= 0 \text{ on } \{ 0\}\times (0,1), \label{CaseMu}\\
(\gamma B_1 - \mu B_2) \mathrm{sin}(\mu ) + \dots\\
\dots +  (\gamma B_2 + \mu B_1) \mathrm{cos }(\mu ) & \text{ for } \mu \not= 0 \text{ on } \{ 1\}\times (0,1), \\
\gamma A_2 - A_1 & \text{ for } \mu = 0 \text{ on } \{ 0\}\times (0,1), \\
\gamma (A_1 + A_2 ) + A_1 & \text{ for } \mu = 0 \text{ on } \{ 1\}\times (0,1) .
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
For $ \mu \not= 0 $ the first two cases of (\ref{CaseMu}) can be combined to give
\begin{equation} \label{NonZeroMuBC}
F(\mu ) =  B_1 \cdot \left((\gamma^2  - \mu^2) \mathrm{sin}(\mu ) + 2 \mu \gamma \mathrm{cos}(\mu ) \right) = 0 .
\end{equation}
The term of the left hand side of (\ref{NonZeroMuBC}) is a function $F$ of $\mu $. Then the zeros of $F$ are solutions of (\ref{NonZeroMuBC}). The following chart shows this function $F$ and its first zeros for $\gamma = 1/2 $.

As one notices, $\mu = 0 $ is a zero of $ F(\mu ) $. Thus there is the question, if $\mu = 0 $ could be admissible for the approach $ X(x) = B_1 \mathrm{sin}(\mu x) + B_2 \mathrm{cos}(\mu x) $. In this case $X$ reduces to $ X(x) = B_2 \mathrm{cos}(\mu x) = B_2 $ and this inserted into (\ref{homogenBC}) is $ \gamma B_2 + 0 = 0 $ which is $ B_2 = 0 $  and $X(x) = 0 $ whenever $ \gamma \not= 0 $. A similar consideration leads to the conclusion that the approach $ X(x) = A_1 x + A_2 $ leads to $A_1 = A_2 = 0$ and again to $ X(x) = 0$. But $X(x) = 0 $ cannot serve as an eigenelement. Thus, we have proved the following theorem:
\begin{theorem}[Eigenvalues are non-zero for $\gamma \not= 0$]
In case of proper Robin boundary conditions, i.e. $ \gamma \not=0 $ the value $ \mu = 0$ is not an element of the spectrum, i.e. it is \emph{not} an eigenvalue. 
\end{theorem}

An observation of the first eigenvalues for the case $\gamma \not= 0 $ may lead to the assumption that each of the eigenvalues $ \{ 6.4382,\, 9.5264,\, 12.6454,\, 15.7713,\dots \} $ have a distance of approximately $ \pi $ to its nearest neighbor. The following result provides a lower bound for the eigenvalues.
\begin{lemma}[Lower estimate for the eigenvalues] \label{lb_eig_vals}
In case of $\gamma \not= 0 $ the eigenvalues $\mu_n $ with $ \mu_n > \gamma $ fulfill $ \mu_n \geq n\cdot \pi $, if $ \mu_0 = 0.9602 $.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Consider the equation 
\begin{equation*}
\left( \gamma^2 - \mu^2 \right) \cdot \mathrm{tan}(\mu ) + 2\mu\gamma = 0 , \text{ or } \mathrm{tan}(\mu ) = -\frac{2\mu\gamma}{\left( \gamma^2 - \mu^2 \right)} .% \left(\right)
\end{equation*}
The solutions are given by
\begin{equation*}
\mu_n = n\cdot\pi + \mathrm{arctan}\left(\frac{2\mu_n\gamma}{\left( \gamma^2 - \mu_n^2 \right)}\right) .
\end{equation*}
Since $\gamma / \mu_n < 1 $ we can use the addition theorem for the arc tangent, cf. \cite[p. 237]{bronstein1980taschenbuch} to get
\begin{equation*}
\mu_n = n\cdot\pi + 2 \mathrm{arctan}\left(\frac{\gamma}{\mu_n}\right) \geq n\cdot\pi ,
\end{equation*}
which proves the assertion.
\end{proof}

In case of $\gamma \not= 0 $ the eigenfunctions are given by
\begin{equation} \label{ONB1}
\phi_n (x) = C_n \cdot \left(\mathrm{sin} (\mu_n x) + \frac{\mu_n }{\gamma } \mathrm{cos} (\mu_n x) \right) \text{ where } n>0 ,
\end{equation}
and $C_n $ so that $ \| \phi_n \|_{L^2} = 1 $. In detail, $C_n $ fulfills
\begin{equation}
C_n = \left(
    \frac{1}{2}
    - \frac{\sin(2\mu_n)}{4\mu_n}
    + \frac{1}{2\gamma}\bigl(1 - \cos(2\mu_n)\bigr)
    + \frac{\mu_n^2}{\gamma^2}
      \left(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{\sin(2\mu_n)}{4\mu_n}\right)
\right)^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}.
\end{equation}

In case of $\gamma = 0 $ the eigenfunctions are given by
\begin{equation} \label{ONB2}
\phi_0 (x) = 1 \text{ and } \phi_n (x) = D_n \cdot \mathrm{cos} (\mu_n x) \text{ where } n>0 ,
\end{equation}
and $D_n = \sqrt{2/\mu_n } $.

Since $ -\Laplace : H_1 \rightarrow H_2 $ is a self-adjoint linear operator between two appropriate Hilbert spaces $H_1 $ and  $H_2 $ the systems (\ref{ONB1}) and (\ref{ONB2}) form an orthonormal basis of $H_1$. A possible and safe choice for the Hilbert spaces is $H_1 = H^{2,2} (0,1 ) $ and $H_2 = L^2 (0,1  ) $. 
As $x$ varies in $(0,1)$ so $y$ does and the consideration done for $x$ can analogously applied to the $y$ variable. So for $ \gamma \not= 0 $ we have
\begin{equation}
\phi_n (y) = C_n \cdot \left(\mathrm{sin} (\mu_n y) + \frac{\mu_n }{\gamma } \mathrm{cos} (\mu_n y) \right) \text{ where } n>0 ,
\end{equation}
with the same numbers $\mu_n $ and $C_n $.

\begin{theorem}[Uniform boundedness of the eigenfunctions $\phi_n $] \label{unibound_eigenf}
For the eigenfunctions $\phi_n $ there is 
\begin{equation*}
% \mathrm{sup}_{(x,y) \in \Omega }  | \phi_{m,n} (x, y)  | \leq 1.1 + 0.3\gamma^2 ,
\mathrm{sup}_{x \in (0,1) } | \phi_n (x)  | \leq \sqrt{1.1 + 0.3\gamma^2 },
\end{equation*}
if the spectrum does not contain zero.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For an arbitrarily fixed $n$ define $ z = 2 \mu_n $, and $ \beta = 2 \gamma $, just for simplicity. Now, let us define the following Laurent polynomial $ p(z) = C_{-1} \frac{1}{z} + p_0 (z ) $ , where $p_0 ( z) = C_0 + C_1 z + C_2 z^2 $, and
\begin{eqnarray*}
C_{-1} & = & - \mathrm{sin}(z), \\
C_0 & = & 1 + \frac{2}{\beta }\left( 1 - \mathrm{cos}(z) \right), \\
C_1 & = & \frac{\mathrm{sin}(z)}{\beta^2 }, \\
C_2 & = & \frac{1}{\beta^2 } .
\end{eqnarray*}
The polynomial $p_0 $ can be estimated as follows 
\begin{eqnarray*}
C_0 & \geq & 1, \\
C_1 z + C_2 z^2 & \geq & \frac{z}{\beta^2 } ( z -1 ),
\end{eqnarray*}
that is $ p_0 (z) \geq 1 + \beta^{-2} z (z-1 ) $. It remains to estimate $ C_{-1} z^{-1} $. In case of homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions the spectrum of the Laplace operator contains the eigenvalue $\mu = z = 0 $ and we cannot estimate anything. In contrast in case of Robin boundary conditions, its spectrum does not contain zero. So, we may assume $ z > 2 \mu_1 = 2\cdot 0.9602 ... = 1.9204 $ which is the double of the first positive zero of $F$ depicted in Figure \ref{fig:MuFunc3}. With $ \mathrm{sin}(2 \mu_1)/(2\mu_1) \approx 0.489225 $ we get $ C_{-1} / z \geq - 0.489225 $. Collecting all together we finally get $ p(z) \geq 1 + \beta^{-2} z (z-1 ) - 0.489225 \geq 1/2 + \beta^{-2} z (z-1 ) $. Note that $ 2 C_n^2 = 1 / p_0 (2 \mu_n )  $ and thus $ C_n \leq 1/2 \cdot (1/2 + \beta^{-2} (2\mu_n) ((2\mu_n)-1 ) )^{-1/2} $. Then we have for the $n$-th eigenfunction the following estimate
\begin{eqnarray*}
\left(\phi_n (x) \right)^2  & = & 2 C_n^2 \cdot \left(\mathrm{sin} (\mu_n x) + \frac{\mu_n }{\gamma } \mathrm{cos} (\mu_n x) \right)^2 \\
 & \leq & \frac{\left(\mathrm{sin} (\mu_n x) + \frac{\mu_n }{\gamma } \mathrm{cos} (\mu_n x) \right)^2 }{\tfrac{1}{2} + (2\gamma^{-2}) \mu_n (\mu_n-1 )} \\
 & \leq & \frac{\left(1 + \frac{\mu_n }{\gamma } \right)^2 }{(2\gamma )^{-2} \mu_n (\mu_n-1 )} \leq 2\frac{1 +  \left(\frac{\mu_n }{\gamma } \right)^2 }{(2\gamma )^{-2} \mu_n (\mu_n-1 )} \\
 & \leq & \gamma^2\frac{1 +  \left(\frac{\mu_n }{\gamma } \right)^2 }{2 \mu_n (\mu_n-1 )} = \frac{\gamma^2 +  \mu_n^2 }{2 \mu_n (\mu_n-1 )} \\
 & = & \frac{\left( \mu_n^2 - \mu_n \right) + \left(\mu_n + \gamma^2 \right)}{2 \mu_n (\mu_n-1 )} \\
 & = & \frac{1}{2} + \frac{ 1}{2 \mu_n-1 } + \frac{\gamma^2 }{2 \mu_n (\mu_n-1 )} \\
 & \leq & \frac{1}{2} + \frac{ 1}{2 \cdot 0.9204 } + \frac{\gamma^2 }{2 \cdot 1.9204 \cdot 0.9204} \\
  & \leq & 0.5 + 0.543... + \tfrac{\gamma^2 }{3.5417...} \leq 1.1 + 0.3\gamma^2 \\
 & \leq & C,
\end{eqnarray*}
that is $ | \phi_n (x)  | \leq \sqrt{1.1 + 0.3\gamma^2 } $, uniformly for all $n \in \mathbbm{N} $. 
\end{proof}
Defining $ \phi_{m,n} (x,y) = \phi_m (x) \cdot \phi_n (y) $ we have an orthonormal basis of $ H^{2,2} (\Omega ) = H^{2,2} (0,1 ) \times H^{2,2} (0,1 ) $.
Then, every function $ f \in H^{2,2} (\Omega ) $ can be written as $ f = \sum_{m,n \in I \times I } (\phi_{m,n}, f)_{L^2 } \phi_{m,n} $, where the index set $I = \mathbbm{N} $ in case of $ \gamma \not= 0 $, and $I = \mathbbm{N} \cup \{ 0 \} $ in case of $ \gamma = 0 $.

\begin{theorem}[Uniform boundedness of the eigenfunctions $\phi_{m,n} $]
For the eigenfunctions $\phi_{m,n} : \Omega = (0,1)^2 \rightarrow \mathbbm{R} $ there is 
\begin{equation*} \label{unibound_eigenf_2d}
\mathrm{sup}_{(x,y) \in \Omega }  | \phi_{m,n} (x, y)  | \leq 1.1 + 0.3\gamma^2 ,
\end{equation*}
if Robin boundary conditions are imposed on the boundary.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
This is a direct consequence of the Theorem \ref{unibound_eigenf}.
\end{proof}

We are now define Green's function as
\begin{equation}
\Green (x,y,x',y',\tau ) = H(\tau ) \sum_{m,n \in I \times I } \phi_{m,n} (x,y) \phi_{m,n} (x', y' ) \cdot \mathrm{exp} (-\alpha (\mu^2_m + \lambda^2_n) \tau ),
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the Heaviside function which is one for $\tau > 0 $ and zero otherwise. With the help of this function a solution $ \wTemp $ of (\ref{homogenHeatC}, \ref{homogenIC}, \ref{homogenBC}) can be constructed as
\begin{equation}
\wTemp (x,y,t)  = \int_0^1 \int_0^1  \Green (x,y,x',y', t ) \wTemp_0 (x',y') \mathrm{d}x' \mathrm{d}y' .
\end{equation} \label{GreenForHomogenHeatC}
Since $ \Laplace \Green = \delta $ in the sense of distributions adding a term $ \int_0^t\int_0^1 \int_0^1  \Green (x,y,x',y',t -t' ) f (x',y') \mathrm{d}x' \mathrm{d}y' \mathrm{d}t' $ to (\ref{GreenForHomogenHeatC}) allows (\ref{homogenHeatC}) to be extended by the function $f$. A solution $\wTemp$ of this inhomogeneous problem is then given by
\begin{eqnarray} \label{GreenForPartiallyInhomogenHeatC}
\wTemp (x,y,t)  & = & \int_0^1\!\int_0^1  \Green (x,y,x',y', t ) \wTemp_0 (x',y') \mathrm{d}x' \mathrm{d}y' \\
& + & \int_0^t\!\int_0^1\!\int_0^1  \Green (x,y,x',y',t -t' ) f (x',y') \mathrm{d}x' \mathrm{d}y' \mathrm{d}t' . \label{GreenForPartiallyInhomogenHeatC2}
\end{eqnarray}

\begin{theorem}[Long-time stability of solutions $w$] \label{w_stability_robin_bc}
The solution $w (x,y, t) $ of the homogeneous problem is uniformly bounded for all $(x,y, t) \in (0,1)^2 \times (0,\infty) $, if Robin boundary conditions are imposed on the boundary.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We consider the integral
\begin{equation*}
I_N (x,y, t) = \int_0^t\!\int_0^1\!\int_0^1  \sum_{m,n =1 }^N \phi_{m,n} (x,y) \phi_{m,n} (x', y' ) \cdot  f (x',y') a_{m,n } (\tau ) \mathrm{d}x' \mathrm{d}y' \mathrm{d}\tau ,
\end{equation*}
with $ a_{m,n } (\tau ) = H(\tau ) \mathrm{exp} (-\alpha (\mu^2_m + \lambda^2_n) \tau ) $ as amplitude function. Since $\sum_{m,n =1 }^N \phi_{m,n} (x,y) \phi_{m,n} (x', y' ) \cdot  f (x',y') $ does not depend on $ \tau $ the integration may be decoupled. % using Fubini's theorem
We then have
\begin{equation*}
\int_0^t a_{m,n } (\tau ) \mathrm{d}\tau = \frac{1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\alpha (\mu^2_m + \lambda^2_n) t } }{\alpha (\mu^2_m + \lambda^2_n)} \leq \frac{1 }{\alpha (\mu^2_m + \lambda^2_n)} \leq \frac{1 }{2\alpha \mu^2_1} .
\end{equation*}
Furthermore, using Theorem \ref{unibound_eigenf_2d} we get
\begin{eqnarray*}
\left| I_N (x,y, t)\right| & = &  \left|\sum_{m,n =1 }^N \frac{1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\alpha (\mu^2_m + \lambda^2_n) t }}{\alpha (\mu^2_m + \lambda^2_n)} \int_0^1\!\int_0^1 \phi_{m,n} (x,y) \phi_{m,n} (x', y' ) \cdot  f (x',y')  \mathrm{d}x' \mathrm{d}y' \right| \\
& \leq & \left( 1.1 + 0.3\gamma^2 \right) \left| \sum_{m,n =1 }^N \frac{1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\alpha (\mu^2_m + \lambda^2_n) t } }{\alpha (\mu^2_m + \lambda^2_n)} \int_0^1\!\int_0^1 \phi_{m,n} (x', y' ) \cdot  f (x',y')  \mathrm{d}x' \mathrm{d}y' \right| .
\end{eqnarray*}
Now, defining $ c_{m,n} = \int_0^1\!\int_0^1 \phi_{m,n} (x', y' ) \cdot  f (x',y')  \mathrm{d}x' \mathrm{d}y' $ note that $ \{ c_{m,n} \}_{m,n \in \mathbbm{N}} $ is a sequence in the sequence space $ \ell^2 $, that means that $ \{ | c_{m,n} | \}_{m,n \in \mathbbm{N}} $ is bounded by a constant $d_1 \in \mathbbm{R} $. Without loss of generality, see Lemma \ref{sturm_liou__eig_vals} we may assume that $\mu^2_m > \gamma $ and $ \lambda^2_n > \gamma $. We then have
\begin{eqnarray*}
\sum_{m,n =1 }^N \frac{ | c_{m,n} | }{\alpha (\mu^2_m + \lambda^2_n)} & \leq & d_1 \sum_{m,n =1 }^N \frac{ 1 }{\alpha (\mu^2_m + \lambda^2_n)} \\
& \leq & d_1 \sum_{m,n =1 }^N \frac{ 1 }{\alpha (m^2 +n^2 )},
\end{eqnarray*}
the last inequality as a consequence of Lemma \ref{lb_eig_vals}.
Furthermore, the right hand side of the last inequality is the partial sum of an absolutely convergent series, i.e. $ \{ 1 / ( m^2 +n^2 ) \} \in \ell^1 $ with limit $d_2 $, and
\begin{eqnarray*}
& & \left| \sum_{m,n =1 }^N \frac{1 }{\alpha (\mu^2_m + \lambda^2_n)} \int_0^1\!\int_0^1 \phi_{m,n} (x', y' ) \cdot  f (x',y')  \mathrm{d}x' \mathrm{d}y' \right| \\
 & \leq & \sum_{m,n =1 }^N \frac{1 }{\alpha (\mu^2_m + \lambda^2_n)} \left| \int_0^1\!\int_0^1  \phi_{m,n} (x', y' ) \cdot  f (x',y')  \mathrm{d}x' \mathrm{d}y' \right| \\
& \leq & \sum_{m,n =1 }^N \frac{1 }{\alpha (m^2 + n^2)} \left| \int_0^1\!\int_0^1  \phi_{m,n} (x', y' ) \cdot  f (x',y')  \mathrm{d}x' \mathrm{d}y' \right| \\
& \leq & d_1\cdot d_2 \in \mathbbm{R} .
\end{eqnarray*}
Since $ 1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\alpha (\mu^2_m + \lambda^2_n) t } \leq 1 $ we finally have
\begin{equation*}
\left| I_N (x,y, t)\right| \leq \left( 1.1 + 0.3\gamma^2 \right) d_1\cdot d_2 ,
\end{equation*}
uniformly for all $N$ and all $(x,y) \in (0,1)^2 $.
That means that the solution $w (x,y, t) $ of the homogeneous problem is uniformly bounded for all $(x,y, t) \in (0,1)^2 \times (0,\infty) $, the statement of the theorem.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
If the homogeneous heat conduction problem is supplemented with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions the assertion of Theorem \ref{w_stability_robin_bc} is not true: It can be shown that the solutions $w$ will tend to infinity as the time $t$ goes to infinity.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The inhomogeneous Heat Conduction Problem}
The full inhomogenous heat conduction problem is given by
\begin{eqnarray} \label{inhomogenHeatC}
\frac{\partial \uTemp }{\partial t } &=& \alpha\Laplace \uTemp + f , \text{ in } \Omega , \\
\uTemp (x,y,0 ) & = & \uTemp_0(x,y) \label{inhomogenIC} , \\
\gamma ( \uTemp - \uTemp_{\mathrm{amb}} ) + \frac{\partial \uTemp }{\partial \nu } &=& 0 \text{ on } \partial \Omega  \label{inhomogenBC} .
\end{eqnarray}
We assume $ \uTemp_{\mathrm{amb}} $ is a constant. With $ \wTemp = \uTemp - \uTemp_{\mathrm{amb}} $ and $ \wTemp_0 = \uTemp_0 - \uTemp_{\mathrm{amb}} $ a solution of the problem $ \partial \wTemp / \partial t = \alpha \Laplace \wTemp + f $ and the conditions (\ref{homogenIC}, \ref{homogenBC}) can be constructed via (\ref{GreenForPartiallyInhomogenHeatC}, \ref{GreenForPartiallyInhomogenHeatC2}). Vice versa, a solution of the inhomogeneous problem (\ref{inhomogenHeatC}, \ref{inhomogenIC}, \ref{inhomogenBC}) can be defined via $ \uTemp = \wTemp + \uTemp_{\mathrm{amb}} $ where $ \wTemp $ is constructed by (\ref{GreenForPartiallyInhomogenHeatC}, \ref{GreenForPartiallyInhomogenHeatC2}) again.

We finish this section with two important result, one for the case of no-flux boundary conditions, the other for the case of Robin boundary conditions:
%
\begin{theorem}[Long-term behavior of the solution of (\ref{HeatEquation2D}) in case of no-flux boundary conditions] \label{LT_no_flux}
With no-flux boundary conditions, i.e. zero normal derivatives of $\uTemp $ at the boundary of $\Omega $ and time-constant bulk heating by internal heat sources $f$ solutions $\uTemp $ of the heat equation (\ref{HeatEquation2D}) will attain inifinite large temperatures when the time $t$ tends to zero.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We consider the time-space integrated version of (\ref{HeatEquation2D}), i.e.
\begin{equation*}
\int_0^t \int_\Omega \frac{\partial \uTemp}{\partial t} \mathrm{d}\volMeas \mathrm{d}t = \alpha \int_0^t \int_\Omega \Delta\uTemp \mathrm{d}\volMeas \mathrm{d}t + \int_0^t \int_\Omega f \mathrm{d}\volMeas \mathrm{d}t .
\end{equation*}
Modifying the left hand side we have
\begin{eqnarray*}
\int_0^t \int_\Omega \frac{\partial \uTemp}{\partial t} \mathrm{d}\volMeas \mathrm{d}t & = &  \int_\Omega \int_0^t \frac{\partial \uTemp}{\partial t} \mathrm{d}t \mathrm{d}\volMeas \\
& = & \int_\Omega \uTemp (t) \mathrm{d}\volMeas - \int_\Omega \uTemp (0) \mathrm{d}\volMeas .
\end{eqnarray*}
Since $f$ does not depend upon time $t$ we obtain
\begin{eqnarray*}
\int_\Omega \uTemp (t) \mathrm{d}\volMeas & = & \int_\Omega \uTemp (0) \mathrm{d}\volMeas + \alpha \int_0^t \int_\Omega \Delta\uTemp \mathrm{d}\volMeas \mathrm{d}t + \int_0^t \int_\Omega f \mathrm{d}\volMeas \mathrm{d}t \\
& = & \int_\Omega \uTemp (0) \mathrm{d}\volMeas + \alpha \int_0^t \int_{\partial \Omega } \frac{\partial \uTemp}{\partial \nu_{\partial\Omega} } \mathrm{d}\surfMeas \mathrm{d}t + t \int_\Omega f \mathrm{d}\volMeas ,
\end{eqnarray*}
where we have applied the Gauss-Green theorem, see \cite[p. 288]{rudin1976} and \cite[p.312]{browder2001}, on $ \int_0^t \int_\Omega \Delta\uTemp \mathrm{d}\volMeas \mathrm{d}t $. Now, using the no-flux boundary condition we have
\begin{eqnarray*}
\mathrm{sup}_{(x,y) \in \Omega } |\uTemp(x,y) | & \geq & \frac{1}{\volMeas (\Omega )} 
\int_\Omega \uTemp (t) \mathrm{d}\volMeas \\
& \geq & \frac{\alpha}{\volMeas (\Omega )} \int_0^t \int_{\partial \Omega } \frac{\partial \uTemp}{\partial \nu_{\partial\Omega} } \mathrm{d}\surfMeas \mathrm{d}t + \frac{t}{\volMeas (\Omega )}  \int_\Omega f \mathrm{d}\volMeas \\
& \geq & \frac{\alpha}{\volMeas (\Omega )} \int_0^t \int_{\partial \Omega } 0\; \mathrm{d}\surfMeas \mathrm{d}t + \mathrm{const } \cdot t \\
& \geq &  \mathrm{const } \cdot t ,
\end{eqnarray*}
that is $ \| \uTemp \|_{L^\infty (\Omega )} =  \mathrm{sup}_{(x,y) \in \Omega } |\uTemp(x,y, t) | $ tends to infinity as $ t\rightarrow \infty $.
\end{proof}

The following important variant of Theorem \ref{w_stability_robin_bc} is now straightforward and -even more important- the \emph{main result} of this paragraph:
\begin{theorem}[Long-time stability of solutions $u$ of (\ref{inhomogenHeatC}, \ref{inhomogenIC}, \ref{inhomogenBC})] \label{u_stability_robin_bc}
The solution $u (x,y, t) $ of the inhomogeneous problem (\ref{inhomogenHeatC}, \ref{inhomogenIC}, \ref{inhomogenBC}) is uniformly bounded for all $(x,y, t) \in (0,1)^2 \times (0,\infty) $.
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
This Theorem \ref{u_stability_robin_bc} warrants that solutions of (\ref{inhomogenHeatC}, \ref{inhomogenIC}, \ref{inhomogenBC}) will not blow up during time, rather they may approach to an equilibrium state. This is important to carry out robust and stable numerical calculations.
\end{remark}

\section{Finite Difference Method}
The ideas of finite difference method is to approximate spatial derivatives by numerical difference quotients. Evaluating a difference quotients at a certain point $(x,y)$ requires its neighboring points $(x-\Delta x,y)$, $(x+\Delta x,y)$, $(x,y-\Delta y)$ and $(x,y+\Delta y)$. For this purpose a set of grid points $\{ (x_i, y_j) \| (i,j) \in I\} $ in $\Omega $. Since $\Omega = ( 0,1 ) \times ( 0,1 ) $ the grid points $\{ (x_i, y_j) $ are aligned with the coordinate axes.
Setting $ h= \Delta x = \Delta y $ we approximate the second order spatial derivatives by
\begin{eqnarray*}
\Delta v(x,y) & \approx & \Delta_h v(x,y) \\
 & = & \frac{1}{h^2}\left(v(x+h, y) -2 v(x,y) + v(x-h,y) \right) \\
& + & \frac{1}{h^2}\left( v(x, y+h) -2 v(x,y) + v(x,y-h) \right),
\end{eqnarray*}
and the first order time derivative by a forward difference
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\mathrm{d} w(t) )}{\mathrm{d} t } \approx \frac{w(t+\tau )}{\tau } .
\end{equation*}
We now use $ \uTemp $ as an alias for $ \Temp $, i.e. $\uTemp = \Temp $.
With a definite $(x,y)$ grid and an initial state $T_0 (x,y) $ at time $t=0$ an approximation for the solution $\uTemp$ at time $t= 0 +\tau $ can be obtained via
\begin{equation*}
\uTemp_1 = \uTemp_0 + \alpha h\Delta_h \uTemp_0,
\end{equation*}
where $ \uTemp_0 = T_0 $ evaluated at grid points. This can be successive be done:
\begin{equation*}
\uTemp_{k+1} = F_{\mathrm{explicit}} (\uTemp_k) = \uTemp_k + \alpha h \Delta_h \uTemp_k,
\end{equation*}
what defines the explicit Euler method. In contrast the implicit method is given via
\begin{equation*}
\uTemp_{k+1} = F_{\mathrm{implicit}} (\uTemp_k, \uTemp_{k+1}) =\uTemp_k + \alpha h \Delta_h \uTemp_{k+1},
\end{equation*}
which requires to solve a system of linear equations to get $\uTemp_{k+1} $, but the numerical behavior of the implicit method is more robust.
Combining explicit and implicit method leads to the Crank-Nicolson method as arithmetic mean of both methods
\begin{equation*}
\uTemp_{k+1} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(  F_{\mathrm{explicit}} (\uTemp_k)  + F_{\mathrm{implicit}} (\uTemp_k, \uTemp_{k+1})  \right) .
\end{equation*}
The stability of the explicit Euler method is limited by the CFL condition:
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\tau }{h^2 } \leq \frac{1}{4\alpha }, 
\end{equation*}
whereas for the Crank-Nicolson method
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\tau }{h^2 } \leq \frac{1}{2\alpha }
\end{equation*}
must be fulfilled for $L^\infty $-stability. For more information, see \cite[p 112]{Ernst2015}.

\section{Physics Informed Neural Networks (PINN)}
\emph{Classification} or \emph{approximation} are the typical applications of neural networks. A short introduction to neural networks can be found in \cite[pp. 809]{stoecker1995formeln}, \cite{rey2011neuralnetworks} and \cite{rojas1993neuronale}. Today the main applications of neural networks contain classification. On the other hand, neural networks can be used to approximate an (unknown) function. Physics informed neural networks are a special type of neural networks to solve mathematical equations of physics and engineering. Typically these equations are ordinary or partial differential equations. Whereas classical numerical solvers outperform this deep learning approach, trained PINN models can be easily reused, if, e.g. a finer spatial grid is needed or the geometry of the spatial domain varies slightly. In good cases these tasks can be done without a new training. Furthermore PINNs can be used to estimate parameters in the equation, if an approximate solution is given, e.g. by measurements. In PINNs the neural network is used as a function approximator. This approach can be found in \cite[Section 3.2]{lopez2008}. The activation functions of a neural network span a function space and a function is approximated by a linear combinations of copies of the activation functions each having different scaling and offset in its function argument. The coefficients, the scalings and offsets are simply weights of the neural network to be adjusted during training. Key ingredient of the learning procedure is the loss function which will be minimized during training. This loss function will be built up by loss terms arising from the mathematical equation to solve.
That means that three loss terms will be added together to give the (total) training loss:
\begin{enumerate}
\item {\bf Physics loss:} The loss arising from deviations of the approximate solution $\hat{\uTemp}$ from the exact solution $u$. Generally, if $F(\uTemp) = 0$ is the mathematical equation, then the physics loss is $L_{\mathrm{Physics}} (w) = |F(w)|^2 $ where $w$ can be any admissible function. In case $w=\uTemp$ the equation $F(u) = 0$ is fulfilled and $L_{\mathrm{Physics}} (\uTemp) = 0 $ is minimal. For any approximate solution $\hat{\uTemp} $ we only have $L_{\mathrm{Physics}} (\hat{\uTemp}) = 0 $. So $L_{\mathrm{Physics}} $ penalizes deviations from the exact solution $\uTemp$.
\item {\bf Initial loss:} Similarly, let $I(\uTemp) = 0$ denote the initial condition. Then the initial loss is defined as $L_{\mathrm{Initial}} (w) = |I(w)|^2 $.
\item {\bf Boundary loss:} Finally, for a boundary operator $B(\uTemp) =0 $ the boundary loss is analogously defined as $L_{\mathrm{Boundary}} (w) = |B(w)|^2 $.
\end{enumerate}
To set up the PINN training the approach of \cite{aryal2024a} is used and extended. The source code used therein can be found in \cite{aryal2024b}. In this thesis an inhomogeneous heat conduction equation in two spatial dimensions with Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions is solved using PINNs. Let us shortly summarize the key features:
\begin{enumerate}
\item {\bf PINN architecture:} A feed forward neural network with five hidden layers, each of them containing 50 nodes is used. The network has input layer with three input nodes corresponding to the variables $x$, $y$, and $t$. The single node of the output layer is assigned to the approximate solution $\hat{u}$. 
\item {\bf PINN Activation function:} All layers except the output layer use the hyperbolic tangent as activation function.
\item {\bf PINN Solver:} To train the PINN the Adam solver of the Pytorch package is used with default settings and a learning rate of 1e-3.
\item {\bf Sampling:} Random samples of $N \in \{ 1000,\, 8000 \} $ points, each for $x$, $y$ and $t$, which is a Monte-Carlo sampling.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Error Measures}
\subsubsection*{PDE Residual}
On the spatial inner points of the numerical solution $ u_h^{(n)} $ at time frame $n$ the discrete Laplace operator $\Laplace_h $ may be applied to give
\begin{equation*}
\Laplace_h u_h^{(n)} (x_i,y_j)  = \frac{u_h^{(n)} (x_{i+1},y_j) + u_h^{(n)} (x_{i-1},y_j) + u_h^{(n)} (x_i,y_{j+1}) +u_h^{(n)} (x_i,y_{j-1}) - 4 u_h^{(n)} (x_i,y_j) }{h^2},
\end{equation*}
for all $n$ and all inner points $(x_i,y_j) $.
The time derivative is just 
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\partial_h u_h^{(n)} (x_i,y_j) }{\partial_h t} = \frac{u_h^{(n + 1)} (x_i,y_j) - u_h^{(n-1)} (x_i,y_j) }{t_{n+1} - t_{n-1}},
\end{equation*}
again for all $n$ and all inner points $(x_i,y_j) $. A local error term now may be given by
\begin{equation*}
E (x_i,y_j) = \left| \frac{\partial_h u_h^{(n)} (x_i,y_j) }{\partial_h t} - \alpha \Laplace_h u_h^{(n)} (x_i,y_j) - f (x_i,y_j) \right|,
\end{equation*}
and its damped version to keep the numerics stable, 
\begin{equation*}
E_{\mathrm{damped}} (x_i,y_j) = \left| u_h^{(n + 1)} (x_i,y_j) - u_h^{(n-1)} (x_i,y_j) - (t_{n+1} - t_{n-1})\cdot\left( \alpha \Laplace_h u_h^{(n)} (x_i,y_j) + f (x_i,y_j)\right) \right|,
\end{equation*}
again for all $n$ and all inner points $(x_i,y_j) $. Possible error measures are now
\begin{enumerate}
\item
Mean absolute error (MAE): Arithmetic mean of all local error terms $E (x_i,y_j) $.
\item 
Damped mean absolute error: Arithmetic mean of all local damped error terms $E_{\mathrm{damped}} (x_i,y_j) $.
\item
Mean square error (MSE): Arithmetic mean of all squared local error terms $E^2 (x_i,y_j) $.
\item
Root mean square error (RMSE): Square root of the MSE.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{remark} \label{Green_derivatives}
The Green function as an exact solution formula allows for a closed form of the derivatives. From the representation (\ref{GreenForPartiallyInhomogenHeatC}, \ref{GreenForPartiallyInhomogenHeatC2}) it is clear that derivatives only apply to Green's function:
\begin{eqnarray*}
\Laplace\Green (x,y,x',y',\tau ) & = & H(\tau ) \sum_{m,n \in I \times I } \Laplace \phi_{m,n} (x,y) \phi_{m,n} (x', y' ) \cdot \mathrm{exp} (-\alpha (\mu^2_m + \lambda^2_n) \tau ) \\
& = & - H(\tau ) \sum_{m,n \in I \times I } (\mu_m^2 + \lambda_n^2) \phi_{m,n} (x,y) \phi_{m,n} (x', y' ) \cdot \mathrm{exp} (-\alpha (\mu^2_m + \lambda^2_n) \tau ),
\end{eqnarray*}
as well as for time derivatives 
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\partial}{\partial \tau } \Green (.,\tau ) = 
\begin{cases}
- \sum\limits_{m,n \in I \times I } \phi_{m,n} (x,y) \phi_{m,n} (x', y' ) \cdot \frac{\mathrm{exp} (-\eta \tau )}{\eta }, & \text{ for } \tau > 0, \\
\mathrm{lim}_{\tau \rightarrow 0+ } \left( - H(\tau )\sum\limits_{m,n \in I \times I } \phi_{m,n} (x,y) \phi_{m,n} (x', y' ) \cdot \frac{\mathrm{exp} (- \eta\tau )}{\eta } \right) , & \text{ for } \tau = 0 ,\\
0, & \text{ otherwise, }
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
where $ \eta = \alpha (\mu^2_m + \lambda^2_n) $.
\end{remark}
%
\begin{remark}
For the solution calculated from Green's function we will use the derivatives given in Remark \ref{Green_derivatives} to calculate the PDE residual. We will not use the finite differences.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark} \label{two_t_frames}
We will use two time frames: 
\begin{itemize}
\item
One with small time steps for the numerical calculation, e.g. inside the finite-difference solvers. Especially the explicit solver must not use too large time steps to keep numerics stable with respect to the CFL condition. We will refer to it as \emph{Calculation Time Grid}.
\item
A time frame with rather large time steps for visualisation and animation. If the time steps here are too small there may no change between two neighbouring times visible. But memory and CPU load would be high for nothing. We will refer to it as \emph{Visualisation Time Grid}.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}
\begin{remark} \label{numerical_dt}
If the coarse time grid of Remark \ref{two_t_frames} is used, the precision of the numerical time derivative may be insufficient. There are some strategies to overcome this artefact:
\begin{itemize}
\item
In view of Remark \ref{two_t_frames} the numerical differentiation wrt to time may lead to more precise results, if the finer time grid is used.
\item
The method involving the Green's function allows for exact time and space derivatives.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}
%
\begin{remark}
For the solutions of the two finite difference solvers we use time derivatives calculated on the \emph{Calculation Time Grid} to compute their PDE residuals.
\end{remark}

\end{enumerate}
For each of these four boundary sections we will calculate the mean square error (MSE) to get a single error value describing the quality of the boundary match of the numerical solution on every boundary section.

\subsection{Case Study Two: Constant initial temperature with Robin boundary conditions}
\subsubsection*{Some Mathematics}
In case 1 the domain $\Omega$ is constantly heated over time where the heat cannot dissipate over across the boundary $\partial \Omega $ due to the no-flux boundary condition imposed on $\partial \Omega $. This leads to permanently ascending temperatures over time without reaching an equilibrium. In real life such an isolated system with permanent constant heat support is not realistic. This can be overcome by either
\begin{itemize}
\item using another heat source which decreases heat supply over time to zero, or
\item imposing other boundary conditions which allow for heat flux over the domain boundary.
\end{itemize}
In this case the latter approach is used by imposing a Robin boundary condition, i.e.
\begin{equation} \label{robin_bc}
a\cdot(\uTemp-\uTemp_0 ) + b\frac{\partial u}{\partial \nu_{\partial \Omega}} = 0,
\end{equation}
where $  \nu_{\partial \Omega} $ is the outer unit normal at the boundary $\partial \Omega $, $\uTemp_0 $ the ambient temperature far from the domain $\Omega $ and its boundary $\partial \Omega $, i.e. far from the thermal boundary layer. The following theorem assures that the solution $\uTemp $ of the heat equation with Robin boundary conditions will tend to a finite equlibrium state if appropriate conditions are fulfilled.

\begin{lemma}[Positiveness] \label{positiveness}
Given the Robin boundary condition
% \begin{equation*}
% h\cdot ( \uTemp - \uTemp_0 ) + \frac{\partial \uTemp}{\partial \nu_{\partial \Omega }} = 0 ,
% \end{equation*}
% one obtains
\begin{equation*}
h w + \frac{\partial w}{\partial \nu_{\partial \Omega }} = 0 .
\end{equation*}
% provided $ w = \uTemp -\uTemp_0 $.
Assume $ \Omega $ is the unit square, i.e. $ \Omega = (0,1)\times (0,1 )$, and $ \uTemp (0) \geq \uTemp_0 $ and $ f \geq 0 $ in $ \Omega $. (no heat sinks in $\Omega $).
Then $ w\geq 0 $ on $ \partial \Omega $ holds.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The boundary conditions read as
\begin{equation*}
h w + \frac{\partial w}{\partial \nu_{\partial \Omega }} = 
\begin{cases}
h w - \frac{\partial w}{\partial x} & \text{ on left boundary where $ x= 0 $, }\\
h w - \frac{\partial w}{\partial y} & \text{ on lower boundary where $ y= 0 $, }\\
h w + \frac{\partial w}{\partial x} & \text{ on right boundary where $ x= 1 $, }\\
h w + \frac{\partial w}{\partial y} & \text{ on upper boundary where $ y= 1 $, }
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
which leads to the following solutions
\begin{equation*}
w(x ,y)  = 
\begin{cases}
C_1(y) \mathrm{exp}(h\cdot x) & \text{ on left boundary where $ x= 0 $, }\\
C_2(x) \mathrm{exp}(h\cdot y) & \text{ on lower boundary where $ y= 0 $, }\\
C_3(y) \mathrm{exp}(-h\cdot x) & \text{ on right boundary where $ x= 1 $, }\\
C_4(x) \mathrm{exp}(-h\cdot y) & \text{ on upper boundary where $ y= 1 $. }
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
Since $ \uTemp (0) \geq \uTemp_0 $ and $ f \geq 0 $ in $ \Omega $ only non-negative functions $C_1 $, $C_2 $, $C_3 $ and $C_4 $ are logically possible. Then $w $ is non-negative n the boundary $ \partial \Omega $.
\end{proof}
\subsubsection*{The full heat conduction problem}
The heat conduction problem under consideration is given by
\begin{equation} \label{heat_conduction_case2}
\frac{\partial \uTemp (x,y,t) }{\partial t } - \alpha \Laplace \uTemp (x,y,t) = f (x,y) \text{ and } \uTemp (x,y,0) = \uTemp_0 (x,y)  \text{ where } (x,y) \in \Omega = (0,1)^2 \text{ and } t \geq 0 .
\end{equation}
We supplement (\ref{heat_conduction_case2}) with Robin boundary conditions:
\begin{equation} \label{heat_conduction_case2_bc}
h \cdot ( \uTemp (x,y,t) -\uTemp_{\mathrm{amb}} ) + \frac{\partial \uTemp (x,y,t)}{\partial \nu_{\partial \Omega }} = 0 \text{ where } (x,y) \in \partial \Omega \text{ and } t \geq 0 .

\subsubsection*{Calculation of the Green function}
%
The Green function given by (\ref{GreenForPartiallyInhomogenHeatC},\ref{GreenForPartiallyInhomogenHeatC2}) is a closed formula to solve the heat conduction equation supplemented with boundary conditions. To calculate the Green's function several integrals have to to be evaluated. To calculate these integrals a bunch of numerical quadrature methods are available four of them are implemented and investigated here:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Simpson rule:} Closed Newton-Cotes of order 2. Used as summarized quadrature rule with weights $ \{ 1/3,\, 4/3,\, 2/3,\, 4/3,\, 2/3, \dots ,\, 2/3,\, 4/3,\,  1/3\} $.
\item \emph{3/8 rule:} Closed Newton-Cotes of order 3. Used as summarized quadrature rule with weights $ \{ 3/8,\, 9/8,\,9/8,\,6/8, \dots ,\,6/8,\, 9/8,\,9/8,\, 3/8\} $.
\item \emph{Milne rule:} Closed Newton-Cotes of order 4. Used as summarized quadrature rule with weights $ \{ 14/45,\, 64/45,\,24/45,\, 64/45,\,28/45, \dots ,\, 64/45,\,24/45,\, 64/45,\, 14/45 \} $.
\item \emph{Clenshaw-Curtis rule:} This method uses an integration grid whose points are not equidistantly distributed. Beyond the calculation of the weights the spatial integration these points are calculated. See \cite{waldvogel2006fast}. 
\item \emph{Closed analytic expression:} Since the heat source does not depend on the time $t$ as well es the eigenmodes integration wrt. time and spatial integration allow for total decoupling. Time integration in (\ref{GreenForPartiallyInhomogenHeatC2}) of the amplitude function can be done exactly and its integral allows the closed form $ \int_0^t \mathrm{exp}\left( -\alpha (\mu^2_m + \lambda^2_n ) \tau \right) \mathrm{d}\tau = \frac{1 -\mathrm{exp}\left( -\alpha (\mu^2_m + \lambda^2_n ) t \right)}{\alpha (\mu^2_m + \lambda^2_n )}$.
\end{itemize}
\begin{itemize}
\item As integration method the summarized Milne rule is used. It is the closed Newton-Cotes formula of order 4. The rule is used separately for both spatial dimensions. We use 5001 sample points in each spatial direction. It turned out that using the first 414 eigenmodes leads to good results in the sense of the PDE residual.
\item A more precise can be done using Clenshaw-Curtis integration see \cite{waldvogel2006fast}.  With 7801 points and 3040 eigenmodes a fairly good PDE residual can be achieved.
\end{itemize}

To compare the four numerical methods we use a spatial grid of 7801 points in x- and y-direction and 3040 eigenmodes. The Gram matrix $G = (g_{i,j})_{i,j=1}^N $ of the eigenfunctions $\phi_n (x) $ is given by $g_{i,j} = \int_0^1 \phi_i (x)\phi_j (x) \mathrm{d}x $. For an orthonormal system $\{ \phi_n (x) \}_{n=1}^N $ we expect a Gram matrix equal to the identity matrix $I$, i.e. $g_{i,j} = \delta_{i,j} $ where $ \delta_{i,j} $ is the Kronecker delta. Then $ G - I $ must be the zero matrix, or numerically $ \mathrm{sup}_{i,j} \| g_{i,j} - \delta_{i,j} \| $. We call this Gram matrix residual. The results are in the following table
\begin{table}[h!] 
\centering
{ \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabular}{ccl}
\toprule
\textbf{Method} & \textbf{PDE residual} & \textbf{Gram matrix residual}\\
\midrule
Simpson & 1.647205e-09 & 6.268031e-09 \\
3/8 rule & 1.428434e-09 & 0.1250000 \\
Milne & 2.068634e-09 & 0.02222222 \\
Clenshaw-Curtis & 1.737587e-09 & 8.719940 e-06 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}}
\caption{Residuals as quadrature quality measures.}
\label{residuals_validation2}
\end{table}
As one observes, 3/8 rule and Milne rule have unexpected large Gram matrix residuals whic are not approximately zero.
Obviously, the Simpson would be a good compromise in view of the two residuals.
